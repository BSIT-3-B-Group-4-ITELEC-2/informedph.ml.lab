PRAGUE, Czech Republic – To expedite the proceedings in Rodrigo Duterte’s case before the International Criminal Court (ICC), the Office of the Prosecutor seeks to admit three pre-recorded witness testimonies regarding the former President’s “narco list.”
In a redacted request for application filed on November 2, Acting Prosecutor Mame Mandiaye Niang requested Trial Chamber III to admit the prior recorded testimonies of witnesses P-1097, P-1170, and P-1318, which are “relevant” proof of Duterte’s “individual criminal liability”, particularly in identifying “high-value targets” on various drug watch lists.
The prosecution added that these statements also establish the Philippine National Police’s (PNP) role in Duterte’s drug war.
Niang stated that the prior recorded testimonies are introduced via the ICC’s Rule of Procedure and Evidence Rule 68(3), which may allow admission of such statements in proceedings while the witness appears in court and does not object.
Recorded testimonies
Although heavily redacted, the three witnesses’ statements, as the prosecution noted, establish the targeting of individuals by PNP officers during Duterte’s drug war, as well as “the shift in [REDACTED] after Mr Duterte became President.”
P-1097’s prior recorded testimony explains the “tokhang” killings and that “warning placards were placed on some victims.”
The prosecution noted that P-1170’s testimony demonstrates Duterte’s public accusation of redacted individuals as being involved with illegal drugs, who were later included on the “PRRD list.”
The pre-recorded testimony of P-1318 is heavily redacted, but the prosecution said it describes the Operation Double Barrel and the tokhang killings, including “some police killings [that] were staged through nanlaban narratives, evidence planting, and cover-ups.”
The prosecution noted that it would conduct a “limited and focused supplementary examination” of the witnesses – all referred to in the filing with male pronouns – should their prior recorded testimonies be introduced as evidence.
It also seeks to use associated materials for P-1097, P-1170, and P-1318 as evidence “necessary to fully comprehend” their accounts.
The filing noted that the three witnesses’ statements “are internally consistent, mutually corroborative, and accompanied by supporting documents in relation to certain aspects,” and that they corroborate the evidence to be provided by other viva voce witnesses.
'Paper-only' witness

In a redacted response filing on the same day, Haynes claims P-1357 “exemplifies the very type of individual whom the Chamber urged the Prosecution to remove from its list.”
“Her testimony is entirely peripheral, as it does not address the substance of [REDACTED] research, the allegations made, or the findings reached. The witness is not even familiar with the events occurring after [REDACTED], having ceased working on the subject following [REDACTED],” Haynes stated.
It may be recalled that the prosecution asked Trial Chamber III on October 19 to admit the prior recorded testimony of P-1357, P-0024, and P-1359 as Rule 68(2)(b) evidence that relates to the alleged systematic policy of “neutralizing” alleged criminals across the Philippines through murder.
The prior recorded testimony of P-1357, referred to in female pronouns, contains details of the existence and operations of the Davao Death Squad and “notes that the words and actions of Mr Duterte indicated his support for killings of criminal suspects.”
But Haynes objected to admitting P-1357’s statement, saying it “adds nothing of substance to the evidence in this case.”
“Further and/or alternatively, the Defence objects to the Prosecution’s improper use of Rule 68(2)(b) to introduce associated material that the witness has neither meaningfully used nor explained, contrary to the Prosecution’s assertions,” Haynes stated.
Haynes said the defense has “no wish” to cross-examine P-1357, “given the marginal relevance of her testimony.”
The defense said it won’t cross-examine P-0024 because of “having already extensively addressed its concerns regarding the introduction of the [REDACTED] dataset into the evidentiary record of these proceedings.”
P-0024’s heavily redacted statement mentions a “legal process,” and “records [that] may have been incomplete or selectively disclosed.” It remains unclear which information P-0024’s statement refers to.
Ramifications, deadlines

EXPLAINER: ICC Rules 68.2 and 68.3: How prior witness testimonies may be used in Duterte trial
THE HAGUE — Rules 68.2 and 68.3 of the International Criminal Court's (ICC) Rules of Procedure and Evidence were mentioned during the fifth and final status conference in the case of former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte on Monday.
The provisions concern the use of previously recorded witness testimony and this could affect how evidence is presented when Duterte's trial begins on November 30.
The key distinction between the two rules is whether the witness must appear before the Trial Chamber.
Under Rule 68.2, the court may allow previously recorded testimony to be introduced even if the witness is not present. This is permitted only under specific circumstances, including when both the prosecution and defense had the opportunity to examine the witness during the recording, or when the witness has died, is unavailable despite reasonable efforts, or has been subjected to improper interference.
The rule also allows the introduction of testimony concerning matters other than the accused's acts and conduct, subject to additional requirements.
Rule 68.3, meanwhile, applies when the witness is present before the Trial Chamber. Previously recorded testimony may be introduced if the witness does not object and the prosecution, defense and judges have the opportunity to examine them during the proceedings.
Atty. Kristina Conti, an ICC counsel representing the victims, explained that Rule 68.3 covers witnesses who already have recorded statements or other material on record.
“Ang ibig sabihin lang po nito, ang may 68.3, ito po ay mga witnesses na meron ng dating testimonya, dokumento o, ah, videos na sila'y nagsasalaysay. So kumbaga may affidavit na dati,” Conti said.
(What this means is that the 68.3 are witnesses who have previous testimonies, documents or, ah, videos of them giving their accounts. So there was an affidavit before.)
Conti said the existence of these materials could help shorten court proceedings because some of the witness's statements and supporting evidence have already been documented.
“Si 68.3, sisipot pa rin (68.3 will still show up),” she said, stressing that witnesses under this provision would still appear before the court.
For Rule 68.2, Conti explained: “Si 68.2, replacement of the testimony. Tanggapin yung testimonya nila pero hindi nasisipot yung witnesses (Their testimony is accepted but they will not appear).”
The use of previously recorded testimony may, however, be contested by the defense, which has raised concerns about its ability to question witnesses whose evidence is significant to the prosecution's case.
The defense has also pointed to the need to examine how certain reports and documents were produced, including the people involved in preparing them. It has indicated that it intends to present its own witnesses in response to the prosecution's evidence.
The Trial Chamber will ultimately decide whether a particular recorded testimony can be admitted under Rule 68 and whether the requirements have been met.
Duterte's trial is scheduled to begin on November 30. — BAP, GMA News

a012
Duterte ICC trial: Victims face language barriers, concerns over scope of charges
THE HAGUE, Netherlands — Victims of alleged crimes committed during former president Rodrigo Duterte's war on drugs face challenges in participating meaningfully in his upcoming trial before the International Criminal Court (ICC), including language barriers, witness protection concerns, and questions over the scope of charges.
Among the key concerns raised during the court's fifth status conference on Monday, November 2, was an ICC decision that could limit the killings covered by the case, potentially excluding some victims from the proceedings.
The conference also addressed preparations for Duterte's trial, scheduled to open on November 30, including witness protection measures and arrangements for victims' participation.
In an interview, attorney Kristina Conti, an ICC Assistant to Counsel, said these issues remain significant for victims as the proceedings approach.
Change in scope of charges raises concerns for victims
Conti said victims' representatives are grappling with the implications of an ICC decision issued on October 9 that appears to limit the scope of killings covered by one of the charges.
The case concerns alleged crimes committed between November 1, 2011, and March 16, 2019, when the Philippines was a member of the ICC. However, the decision reportedly limits the killings covered by one charge to the period between July 1, 2016, and September 30, 2018.
“Yung tinatawag na paglimita ng scope ang problema ngayon ng mga biktima kasi posibleng hindi lahat, eh, pumasok doon sa parameters,” Conti told GMA News Online.
(The limitation of the scope is now a problem for victims because some of them may not fall within the parameters of the case.)
Conti said victims' representatives are still trying to understand the implications of the ruling, arguing that the alleged crimes should be considered within the full period of the Philippines' membership in the ICC.
“Dapat hanggang 2019 na miyembro yung Pilipinas ng ICC. Tapos dapat hanggang 2022 kung kailan tsaka pa lang natapos yung term ni Duterte,” she said.
(It should cover when the Philippines was a member of the ICC, until 2019. And Duterte's term only ended in 2022.)
The potential narrowing of the scope is a concern for victims who fear that not all alleged crimes may be considered in the proceedings.
Participating victims need not testify
Conti clarified that victims participating in the proceedings do not necessarily have to take the witness stand. Their role is to express their views and concerns through their common legal representatives and, potentially, seek reparations if Duterte is convicted.
She stressed that the number of participating victims does not determine the strength of the prosecution's evidence.
“Yung pagliit o paglaki ng bilang na ito ay hindi nakakaapekto directly doon sa kaso or doon sa strength ng evidence. Pero siyempre, ibang tulong din yun na alam ng prosecution, makikita nila na ganito karami ang gustong mag-participate,” she said.
(The increase or decrease in the number of participating victims does not directly affect the case or the strength of the evidence. But, of course, it also helps the prosecution to see how many people want to participate.)
Conti encouraged victims to participate even if they are unwilling or unable to testify, submit affidavits, or recount their experiences in court.
“Pero gusto pa rin sana namin na anyayahan sila na mag-participate. Not necessarily para maging witness, not necessarily para magbigay ng statement na kumbaga affidavit, pero para magbigay ng views and concerns, mga pananaw at mga agam-agam,” she said.
(We still want to encourage them to participate. Not necessarily as witnesses, and not necessarily to submit affidavits, but to share their views and concerns.)
Applications from participating victims will continue to be accepted until the prosecution finishes presenting its evidence, Conti said.
The prosecution has been allotted 150 hours to present its evidence, a phase she estimates could take approximately a year.
Victims' safety and participation
Conti said preparations for the November 30 trial were largely in place, with the court emphasizing protective measures for witnesses who will testify either in person or through audio-visual links.
“Pero yung maganda nga dito na na-emphasize ay yung protective measures para sa witnesses,” she said.
(What was particularly good was the emphasis on protective measures for witnesses.)
These measures could include withholding witnesses' identities and preventing them from appearing on screen if the ICC determines that revealing their identities could put them at risk.
“Posibleng hindi rin natin makita sa screen. Posibleng boses lang ang marinig natin o kaya translated voice,” said Conti.
(We may not even see them on screen. We may hear only their voice, or a translated voice.)
She said protecting victims and witnesses is particularly important given the trauma they may experience when recounting alleged abuses.
Filipino language request faces hurdles
Another issue raised by victims' representatives was the request to use Filipino during portions of the proceedings, particularly when reading statements directly from victims.
Conti said the request was not simply about language preference but about allowing victims to hear their experiences conveyed in their own words.
“Hindi sila magsasalita ng Tagalog simple dahil gusto nila, kundi dahil meron silang i-quote o babasahin na statement diretso directly from the victims,” she said.
(They are not asking to speak in Tagalog simply because they want to, but because they intend to quote or read statements directly from the victims.)
However, the request appears unlikely to be granted, meaning statements would have to be delivered in English or translated.
Conti said this would not affect the legal merits of the case but could influence victims' perception of whether they are genuinely included in the proceedings.
“Pero yung impression lang sa Pilipinas, yung participation ng victims. Kumbaga eh, isinali niyo na nga kami... Pero kailangan pala English dito,” she said.
(But it affects the impression in the Philippines about victims' participation. It's as if we're being told, ‘You've included us, but everything has to be in English.’)
She warned that the English-language requirement could discourage some victims from participating, particularly those without immediate access to lawyers or legal representatives.
“Isa siyang malaking barrier—lalo na sa iba na hindi naman naaabot kaagad nung CLRV o kaya nakakapag-consult agad sa mga abogado, so baka maiwan sila sa proseso o hindi nila maintindihan ang nangyayari.”
(It is a significant barrier, especially for those who cannot immediately reach a common legal representative or consult a lawyer. They could be left behind in the process or may not understand what is happening.)
Duterte's trial is scheduled to begin on November 30. He faces three counts of crimes against humanity in connection with alleged killings during his administration's anti-drug campaign. — BM, GMA News

PAGASA sees 1-2 tropical cyclones in PAR in November 2026
One or two tropical cyclones are expected to enter or form within the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR) in November 2026, according to the state weather bureau PAGASA.
In a report on Balitanghali on Monday, PAGASA noted that historically, tropical cyclones entering in November typically make landfall over the Bicol Region, Eastern Visayas, or the Caraga Region.
Forecast tracks indicate that these tropical cyclones may also traverse the Visayas toward Palawan or cut across Central Luzon, though there remains a chance they could recurve or veer away without making landfall.
Earlier, PAGASA stated that up to four tropical cyclones could still enter or form inside the PAR before the end of 2026, while two or more tropical cyclones may enter between January and April 2027.
According to the bureau’s climate outlook, most parts of the Philippines are expected to experience way below-to-below-normal rainfall in November 2026, except for Davao City, Davao del Norte, and Agusan del Norte, where near-normal rainfall conditions are expected.
From December 2026 through January 2027, PAGASA forecasts way below-to-below-normal rainfall across the country.
El Niño
The effects of a strong El Niño are expected to prevail across the country toward the end of 2026 and persist into the first quarter of 2027.
According to PAGASA, at least 12 areas in the Philippines are forecast to experience drought this November as the strong El Niño persists.
While El Niño is typically associated with dry conditions and hot weather, the state weather bureau explained that historically, more tropical cyclones reach typhoon or super typhoon intensity during El Niño events, regardless of whether the phenomenon is classified as weak, moderate, strong, or very strong.
PAGASA Climate Monitoring and Prediction Section Chief Ana Liza Solis explained that warmer ocean temperatures could still create favorable conditions for tropical cyclone development.
“Dahil napakainit nga po ng temperatura ng karagatan na pinagmumulan ng mga tropical cyclone development, so favorable pa rin po siya na magkaroon ng mga bagyo and historically po, ang mga possible po ay mga typhoon and super typhoon category,” Solis said.
(Because the seas where tropical cyclones develop are very warm, conditions remain favorable for cyclone development, and historically, typhoons and super typhoons are possible.)
Solis noted that El Niño does not eliminate the possibility of rain.–NB, GMA News

Kiko Pangilinan urges crackdown on hoarders, profiteers amid El Niño threat
Senate Agriculture Committee chair Francis “Kiko” Pangilinan on Monday urged government agencies to intensify their crackdown on hoarding and profiteering as the looming Super El Niño threatens food production and prices.
In a statement, Pangilinan said enforcement of existing laws, particularly Republic Act No. 7581 or the Price Act, should not be limited to small vendors but should also target major business operators and those behind illegal price manipulation.
“I call on implementing agencies, local price councils, and law enforcement authorities to gather evidence and pursue criminal and administrative cases, where warranted, against those responsible for illegal price manipulation,” Pangilinan said.
“Kung maliliit na tindero lamang ang hahabulin, walang saysay ito. Dapat nating panagutin ang mga malalaking negosyante, mga may-ari, at mga utak sa likod ng mga sindikatong sangkot sa hoarding at manipulasyon ng presyo,” he added.
(If we only go after small-scale vendors, it’s pointless. We must hold major business owners, proprietors, and the masterminds behind syndicates involved in hoarding and price manipulation accountable.)
Section 5 of the Price Act prohibits hoarding, profiteering, and the formation of cartels involving basic necessities and prime commodities.
Violators face imprisonment of five to 15 years and a fine ranging from P5,000 to P2 million.
Pangilinan also urged the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI), Department of Agriculture (DA), Department of Health (DOH), and the Price Coordinating Council to strengthen market monitoring and enforce price freezes in areas placed under a state of calamity due to El Niño.
“Hindi na dapat pagkakitaan ang nalalapit na kalamidad na magpapahirap pa lalo sa ating mga kababayan, lalo na sa mga magsasaka at mangingisda na mamimili rin sa palengke. Sa panahon ng krisis, dapat protektado ang mga mamimili laban sa pagsasamantala,” he said.
(We should not profit from the impending calamity that will further burden our countrymen, especially farmers and fisherfolk who also buy food at the market. In times of crisis, consumers must be protected from exploitation.)
Section 6 of the Price Act provides for an automatic price freeze on basic necessities at their prevailing prices when an area is placed under a state of calamity or emergency, among other circumstances.
Unless lifted earlier by the President, the price freeze remains in effect for the duration of the condition that triggered it, but for no more than 60 days.— MCG, GMA News

WTO flags high trade costs, regulatory hurdles in PH
GENEVA, SWITZERLAND — The Philippines needs to address non-tariff barriers to reduce trade costs and strengthen its competitiveness in global supply chains, including the semiconductor industry, World Trade Organization (WTO) Secretariat officials said.
Asked which reforms the Philippines should prioritize, a WTO official identified non-tariff barriers, such as import licensing and labeling requirements, which can increase the cost of trade and doing business in the country.
“Non-tariff barriers, I would say,” the official said, noting, however, that the Philippines had already made progress in investment and tariff reforms.
The official added that the Philippine government would ultimately decide which reforms to prioritize.
The recommendation comes as the WTO Secretariat’s 2026 Trade Policy Review: Philippines cited estimates showing that the country's trade costs are 20% higher than the ASEAN average.
WTO data show that the country's simple average most-favored-nation (MFN) applied tariff rate stood at 5.9% in 2025, with only 13.6% of imports entering duty-free under MFN terms. Its trade-weighted MFN average was 6% in 2024.
Other measures that add to trade costs include pre-border technical verification, cross-border electronic invoicing and a national single window that has yet to become fully operational, according to the officials.
They also cited fragmented regulations and logistics pass-through fees, partly attributable to the country's geography and 7,641 islands, as factors that could drive up trade costs. However, they cautioned against interpreting the figures as an indication that the Philippines was falling behind its regional peers.
“Lowering costs and making the economy more attractive to do business with is work for any country,” another official said in an interview at WTO headquarters.
The official also said the Philippines could benefit from strengthening industry clusters and training workers for semiconductor-related jobs to deepen its participation in global value chains.
The country could also capitalize on its strength in services exports, particularly business process management services.
“You need to specialize, so you specialize in what you're good at. The Philippines is good at providing services, and definitely that's how you earn your money. But at the same time, the Philippines has definitely more than services,” the official said.
For his part, Philippine Ambassador and Permanent Representative to the WTO Manuel Antonio Teehankee said the country should address infrastructure and logistics concerns to help reduce trade costs.
“Improved infrastructure for inter-island transport and rail is very important for moving goods inside the country because import-export is also dependent on internal logistics and warehousing. So that's the other big area of infrastructure that we should develop,” he said in a separate interview.
Latest data from the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) show that net inflows of foreign direct investments (FDI) stood at US$689 million in July, down 44.1% from US$1.23 billion in the same month last year but up 54.1% from the US$447 million recorded in June.
Meanwhile, the Board of Investments (BOI) approved P900.06 billion worth of investments from January to September, up 53.05% from the same period last year and equivalent to 90% of its P1-trillion target for the year.
Domestic investors accounted for 89.88%, or P808.96 billion, of the approved investments, while foreign investors accounted for P90.40 billion.
The Philippines is also seeking to capitalize on the artificial intelligence (AI) boom, with the country set to host the US-led Pax Silica hub on a 1,600-hectare site within the 9,450-hectare New Clark City in Tarlac. The initiative is part of efforts to build an allied supply-chain network for critical technologies and minerals.

GENEVA, Switzerland — The Philippines and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) should diversify their trade markets and shift away from heavy reliance on the United States for exports and China for supplies, a top official of the World Trade Organization (WTO) said.

“The problem, the issue with ASEAN is they are highly dependent on the US market. Highly. So my message to ASEAN is diversify, diversify, diversify,” she told reporters in a briefing at the WTO headquarters.
“You know, you have many Asians who are in Latin America. You have a huge Japanese population in Brazil and in Peru. You have Filipinos everywhere, so why are you not (diversifying?)” she added.
Okonjo-Iweala also cited trading opportunities in Africa, which Thailand has included in its long-term plans because of the continent’s growing labor force, as well as in resource-rich Central Asian countries such as Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan in Central Asia, and Armenia, and Azerbaijan in South Caucasus.
The Philippines’ reliance on major trading partners is seen in the latest trade figures, as the United States accounted for more than a quarter or 26.6% of exports in September, while South Korea and China together accounted for nearly half or 48.4% of imports, according to the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA).
Total trade in goods for the month reached $24.903 billion in September, with imports exceeding exports by $5.154 billion. The latest trade gap compares with the $4.293-billion deficit in August, and the $4.672-billion deficit in September 2025.
The areas cited by Okonjo-Iweala — Africa, Central Asia, and Latin America — were not among the Philippines’ leading trade partners in September, highlighting the potential to expand into such markets.
“My message to them (ASEAN) is to diversify your market supplies because you’re very dependent on China. Diversify your market for your goods because you’re very dependent on the US,” she said.
Her remarks come a month before the Philippines is set to host the 49th ASEAN Summit, with US President Donald Trump confirmed to attend.

“Growth in AI-enabling goods has been even stronger than expected, and that’s the main impetus behind that. The other thing is economies have been more resilient to rising energy prices than perhaps many expected,” he added.
For 2027, however, the WTO expects Asian merchandise trade volume growth to decelerate to 5.2%, but still faster than the 3.2% forecast made in March.
“When you have above-average growth in one year, it tends to be somewhat weaker the next year. There’s a lot of uncertainty about 2027 generally, just because of energy prices and the durability of the AI investment boom,” Nee said.
“It could go on for several years. The Middle East conflict could be resolved tomorrow. We don’t have a crystal ball. These are probabilistic estimates based on data available at the moment,” he added.

One strait, one tank, one drought
One strait, one tank, one drought
Every Tuesday morning, a rider I will call Jun fills his tank before the sun is up. He does it on Tuesday because that is the day the new pump price takes effect, and he has learned to read Monday’s news the way a farmer reads the sky. This week the news says gasoline goes up again by two to three pesos a liter. Three pesos a liter, ten liters a day, 30 pesos gone before his first passenger. Multiply that by every jeepney, tricycle and fishing boat in the country, and you see how a war in the Middle East ends up in a lunchbox in Caloocan.
This has been the hardest year for fuel in living memory. In March, when the Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz, diesel climbed past P130 a liter and gasoline past P110. The President declared a national energy emergency. Since January, gasoline has risen by a net of about P68 a liter and diesel by about P65. Jeepney drivers have gone on strike at least four times. Government helped with a fuel discount for jeepneys and a tax cut on LPG and kerosene, but the minimum jeepney fare has gone up by only one peso twice. Fuel rose by 60 and fares rose by two. That gap is why drivers are angry, and they are right to be.
Here is the part that should bother all of us. The Philippines imports nearly all of its oil, and most of it passes through that one strait. In the nearly 30 years since we deregulated the oil industry, the government has never owned a single storage tank for a bad day. Our fuel inventory, about 53 days today, sits in private facilities and mostly belongs to private companies. In a real emergency, the state has very little of its own to release.
That is finally changing, and it deserves more credit than it is getting.
Energy Secretary Sharon Garin has been doing the unglamorous work. Last week she told Reuters the Philippines is negotiating with Saudi Arabia, Russia, the United States, Japan and others to store oil here, and that the government will start building its first state-owned tank in Bataan next year, beginning with one million barrels and growing toward a 60-day reserve.
In the House, lawmakers have filed a bill to create a Philippine Strategic Petroleum Reserve so the stockpile is written into law and does not depend on who sits in the department. For once, the executive and Congress are pulling in the same direction.
It is late, but it is right, and the secretary should be given room to finish the job rather than be asked at every hearing why it was not done 20 years ago.
But a tank is only half the story. The other half is water.
Next year is an El Niño year, which means less rain, lower rivers and hazier skies. Secretary Garin has said our hydropower output could fall by as much as 60 percent in the first half of 2027 and solar by 20 percent. When the dams run low and the panels dim, the grid turns to diesel and bunker fuel to keep the lights on. So the same drought that empties our reservoirs will send us back to the oil market at exactly the moment it is most dangerous.
We saw a preview in 2024, when El Niño gave the Luzon grid 11 red alerts and 31 yellow alerts in one summer. In 2027 we may get the drought and the war at the same time.
A country that plans for one crisis at a time keeps getting surprised. A serious plan has four parts, and none of them requires a miracle.
First, pass the reserve law before the Bataan tank is filled. A stockpile that exists only in an executive program can be sold, borrowed against or quietly forgotten by the next administration. Put it in statute. Say how many days it must hold, who can release it and when.
Second, treat water like fuel. El Niño is a water crisis before it is an energy crisis. A dam that holds water through the dry months is a strategic reserve too, and deserves the same urgency we are finally giving to oil.
Third, buy from more than one place. Every barrel that does not have to pass through Hormuz is insurance.
Fourth, reduce how much we need. Filipino households have quietly become some of the world’s biggest buyers of solar panels because they are tired of paying some of the highest power rates in Asia.
I say this as someone whose company lives and dies by the price of gasoline. Every household that makes its own power is one less that waits on Monday’s news with dread.
None of this is cheap. A full reserve has been estimated at more than P150 billion, and critics will fairly ask whether that money should go to grids and renewables instead. I would fund both.
Every price spike is already a tax, collected by foreign producers and paid by Filipino commuters, with nothing left behind. A reserve, a dam, a charging network. These are taxes we pay to ourselves, and they stay.
Jun does not care whether the tank is in Bataan or Singapore. He cares whether, on a bad week in 2027, the pump price moves by one peso instead of 10. That is what a reserve buys. Not cheap fuel forever, but a buffer between a missile strike and a man’s dinner table.
We are finally building the reserve. Let us write it into law, and build the water reserve beside it, before the dry season reminds us why.
The best time to dig the well was before the drought. The second best time is now.

The myth of the ‘perfect victim’
The myth of the ‘perfect victim’
In the month leading to International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, which we commemorate on Nov. 25, an allegation of sexual assault at a prestigious university has re-ignited discussion about the safety of women, the requirements of consent and the rape myths that create the idea of the “perfect victim.” The incident concerning the so-called “Cornell 7” reached international news upon the filing of a civil case by the alleged victim, who claimed that seven fraternity members allegedly drugged and sexually assaulted her over several hours in October 2024. The civil lawsuit, coupled with other reports as well as statements from Cornell – which had conducted its own investigation after the events, which is also currently under scrutiny – allows a timeline to be created that shows the nature of the circumstances as we know it surrounding the incident in October 2024:
The woman who filed the suit, referred to as Jane Doe, drank alcohol at her sorority house and at the bar where the fraternity party was taking place, where she was allegedly served alcohol despite saying she was underage. Later that night she arrived at the fraternity house “visibly intoxicated” and says that she was pressured into taking ketamine by two fraternity brothers who then propositioned a threesome. Afterwards, one of the men allegedly sends a message to the fraternity’s Snapchat group of more than 50 men, suggesting that she was available for “free” sex; another member later asked “Shop still open?” and received the response, “Yea.”
Multiple fraternity members allegedly show up and rape her over several hours, even “pouring lines of Ketamine all over her body and snorting them,” before the woman loses consciousness. The woman sent some apparently friendly texts to members of the fraternity for a few days after, but that quickly changes and around two weeks later she reports the incident to the campus police, where she states that “I can say with 100 percent confidence I was raped” – but it is now known that the campus police omitted this in the report they sent to the prosecutor.
Cornell University commenced what is known as a Title IX investigation, holding multiple hearings over a four-week period, and eventually two of the accused were expelled, two suspended for at least two semesters, one had already graduated before any punishment could be handed down and two were found not to be responsible for sexual misconduct. Allegedly, at least some of the accused were directed to undergo training and “write reflective essays” and none of the accused are criminally prosecuted. However, Jane Doe was not informed of the results of the investigation. She filed her civil lawsuit in September this year.
The allegations made by Jane Doe and the failure of institutional action that followed is horrific to me and to many others, and yet while much of the reaction to the case was of condemnation and calls for action, the circumstances attendant to the event and all that surrounded it provided, unfortunately, fertile ground for victim blaming and a regurgitation of myths and misconceptions about rape and consent.
Those who would excuse the assault point to Jane Doe’s initial willing consumption of alcohol as somehow disqualifying her claim to have been mentally incapacitated by drugs or drink – a position that has been codified into law in certain places, including New York where the alleged assault took place.
As happens all too frequently, there was also no shortage of people that went after the character of Jane Doe herself – the differing tenor of her accounts of what happened, or her choice to drink or attend the party in the first place, such as an opinion piece in USA Today which ended with the line: “Regretting one’s bad decisions is a far cry from rape.” The persistence and prevalence of these opinions – and the belief that something is not “real rape” unless it adheres to strict conditions, which include the pristine virtue of the victim – show how much work still has to be done to explain the concept of consent and the inherent and equal dignity of women.
A woman is not property, to be passed around or offered to others like a bag of chips. A woman’s refusal is not of less value than a man’s request, nor can it be presumed to encompass more than anything explicitly said. A woman’s right to her body is in no way lessened by what she wears, by whom she has chosen to sleep with in the past, by her looks, job, social class or disposition.
A helpful acronym that has been used to understand the nature of consents is that of F-R-I-E-S. Consent must be:
• Freely given; not a product of deception, intoxication, coercion or pressure;
• Reversible at any time; a yes only lasts until it becomes a no, there is no such thing as “touch move;”
• Informed; it must be given with full knowledge of what is being agreed to, and what was agreed to cannot be changed without consent being required anew;
• Enthusiastic; uncertainty is not consent, nor is acquiescence because of expectation or peer pressure – it must come from a genuine desire to say yes.
• Specific; there is no room for hidden meanings – a yes to a date is not a yes to sex, a yes to sex is not a yes to film it.
What makes the Cornell 7 case resonate with so many is not because it is rare, it is because that such treatment of women and the manner by which these acts are defended is all too common, and a sign of the underlying misogyny that must be rooted out.
The attitude that allows men in private groups to talk about women like property is the same that leads to men trafficking Filipino women as “mail-order-brides,” then forcing them to work as prostitutes. These attitudes are not harmless, or traditional, or something that can be excused by “boys will be boys.”
Boys, and girls, will be how we raise them to be. It is our job to raise them to understand consent, women’s equality and basic human dignity. Or else acts of violence against women will remain commonplace, and that is unacceptable.
Consent and sexual education must be a priority of our schools, and schools and workplaces both must have zero tolerance for cultures of misogyny.

Big Boy’s hot pitch: A nationwide solar/RE ‘power bank’
Big Boy’s hot pitch: A nationwide solar/RE ‘power bank’
Don’t we all love a reliable and handy power bank, especially when our mobile phone is almost running on empty and we don’t have access to electricity just yet or, worse, when an outage suddenly hits?
Now imagine something like that but on a grand scale – a battery energy storage system (BESS) that could help power millions of households all over the country, especially now that we’re experiencing an energy crisis.
Tycoon Henry “Big Boy” Sy Jr. is pitching this as a fast, practical and doable solution to the country’s power woes: allowing the National Grid Corp. of the Philippines to put up a chain of BESS facilities nationwide which NGCP could tap when certain areas don’t have enough power supply.
Project Airbag
Big Boy, vice chairman of NGCP, remained in his trademark cool and calm demeanor, but could not conceal the urgency of the situation as he discussed his proposal during a rare media chat last week.
Actually, you’d know a situation is bleak, even desperate, and already taking a heavy toll on the economy when tycoons start speaking out and pitching their own solutions.
Big Boy wasted no time and went straight to his point – the power crisis and how to solve it.
He calls it Project Airbag, perhaps because we are in an emergency and his proposal can provide fast-response storage, which in turn would strengthen grid flexibility, resilience and energy access.
Under Project Airbag, NGCP would put up an initial pipeline of 1,850 megawatts of BESS capacity. Of this, 850 MW are already ongoing in six sites across the country and 1,000 MW for 12 sites still for assessment. In the next five years, this could reach 10,000 MW.
A battery storage stores available electricity and releases it when the system is under pressure. It gives NGCP an additional transmission-side tool to support the system during short-duration shortages.
Putting up BESS facilities is faster than new generation can be built – less than a year, Big Boy said, compared to a power plant which could take up to seven years, sometimes even longer, to build.
The project is intended to help address immediate system pressures, prepare the grid for tighter supply conditions associated with the 2027 El Niño and provide longer-term flexibility as the country’s generation mix continues to evolve.
Big Boy said the priority is the Visayas, which is currently experiencing blackouts because of tight supply conditions and simultaneous generator outages.
A wider grid for 2027 El Niño
The battery rollout, however, is not limited to the Visayas, Big Boy said.
El Niño, he said, poses a risk to the whole country as it creates a foreseeable risk of reduced hydroelectric output, particularly in areas where generation depends heavily on rainfall and reservoir levels. The risk is national, which supports the deployment of storage in other parts of the grid as well.
Batteries can then work during periods of reduced hydro output or other system stress conditions.
In all, Big Boy said, Project Airbag is a practical intervention that NGCP can pursue within its own sphere while longer-term supply solutions are still being developed.
It’s not supposed to be a threat to generation companies because batteries do not create electricity and cannot substitute for adequate generation over prolonged periods.
The underlying structural requirement remains sufficient, dependable power generation.
The deployment is therefore intended as a proactive resilience measure rather than a reaction after supply conditions have already deteriorated.
Power producers react
During the chat, I told Big Boy that power generators may not like his idea.
True enough, the Philippine Independent Power Producers Association Inc. (PIPPA) immediately raised concerns on the proposal.
PIPPA said its concern is primarily who owns these batteries and how their cost is recovered, not with the purpose of the BESS itself.
“By the description alone, supplying energy during shortages and peaks and providing fast-response reserve support are the services that generation companies and BESS operators already offer competitively in the Wholesale Electricity Spot Market and under power supply agreements and ancillary services procurement agreements.
“PIPPA respectfully submits that NGCP ownership of such facilities, with their cost recovered through regulated transmission rates, runs contrary to the structure and intent of Republic Act 9136, the Electric Power Industry Reform Act of 2001, and to the DOE’s policy on energy storage systems,” PIPPA said in a position paper.
Reacting to this, Big Boy said laws were intended to help the people.
The NGCP, in a separate briefing paper on Project Airbag, likewise said that new generation projects take significantly longer to develop, finance, approve, construct and commission.
Airbag can provide a bridge during that development period by reducing immediate system vulnerability.
It’s really short-term transmission relief supporting – not replacing – long-term generation investment.
In a way, Big Boy said, it’s about democratizing access to energy, given that the current supply challenge is acute and pervasive.
Additional supply, he said, must therefore come from as many viable sources as possible.
The objective is not to concentrate or monopolize participation in the energy sector but to disperse the burden of addressing the country’s supply requirements by enabling more participants and resources to contribute, NGCP said.
“We really have to do something before it’s too late,” he said.
Will electricity prices go down? Big Boy is confident that it will.
And for that alone, it’s worth a try.

Symbiosis
Symbiosis
After all the country suffered in the wake of the flood control scandal, the congressmen are at it again. Funds from regular public services were again shifted to pet projects distributed according to congressional districts.
The Supreme Court boldly ruled unconstitutional the practice of pushing regular expenditures to the “unprogrammed” category, thereby ensuring funding for pet projects. Undeterred, the congressmen repeated the scheme that had the effect of inflating the budget total.
It is not that our legislators learned nothing from the monumental infra scandal that continues taking a deadly toll on our economy. It is that they need to skim from the budget to survive the coming election year.
Three decades ago, Jose Almonte, then national security adviser to former president Fidel Ramos, aptly described our politics. He said we have a “pork barrel state” and this is the root of all the evil that traumatizes us.
We have a weak political party system for a reason. Political allegiance is not built on shared adherence to a program of government or a vision of the country’s future. Political support is mustered through transactional politics. Which is to say money politics.
Without effectively functioning political parties, elections are won on the basis of sheer name-recall in a contest were all contenders promise a bottomless cornucopia of state- sponsored social goods. Reliance on name-recall opens the way to celebrity politics. The political economy for this is fairly straightforward: the better known the candidate, the less campaign resources are necessary to build recall.
Our weak political party system is, therefore, a measure of the vitality of transactional politics. Voters are loyal to a candidate, not any party program.
Once elected, the winning candidate is expected to deliver patronage goodies to his voters. This is the popular notion of “public service.” It is not to build the nation. It is to distribute the subsidies.
Transactional politics is expensive. Constituencies expect to be pampered in between elections. Voters expect to be courted by the politicians. They expect elected officials to broker the delivery of services in their favor. This is why politicians are involved by way of guarantee letters for patients even if we have, in principle, universal health care. Elected officials are supposed to have financial aid ready for everyone in need.
Trapped in transactional politics, governments tend to spend way more than they earn. A pork barrel state cannot be fiscally prudent. Because of the nature of our politics, the Philippine government is driven into unsustainable debt.
We have seen the recent riots in France. With an outstanding debt that is bigger than its economy, the French government has lost the fiscal headroom to fulfill its end of the presumed social contract. With an aging population, government finds it more politic to spend for pensioners than invest in education. Thus the riots among the young.
This is an explosive situation. It repeats itself in many other countries where distinct constituencies compete for a larger share of the public spending pie. Whether in Kathmandu, in Jakarta or in Paris, the increasingly difficult economic conditions will make the young feel disenfranchised.
In our own case, the budget debate has the potential to spark unrest.
The House of Representatives hijacked the budget. They took money out of foreign-assisted projects, education and health to make way for 7,000 petty district projects. This is, of course, an election-year budget.
But in hijacking funds for strategic infrastructure, such as the commuter rail system, the congressmen’s interest run against the interest of the section of the elite that depends on national constituencies. The executive branch wants funding for its legacy projects restored. The senators will want funding for health and education left intact. In a season of scarcity, a battle looms between those who need to keep their home constituencies happy and those who want the agencies of national government adequately funded.
We have been hearing a lot of brave talk from senators about purging the 2027 national budget of all the insertions made by the Lower House – including that fantastic infra fund set aside for the President’s son. We will see next week, when plenary deliberation happens at the Senate, if the senators find the courage to wipe away all the distortions in the House version of next year’s budget.
The pork barrel state will fight back. They will remind the ruling faction that the congressmen’s ability to deliver the votes depends so much on their getting their way with the budget allocations.
The camp of Marcos Jr. might not be ready to wage a budget war against the congressmen. These district-level politicians, after all, effectively constitute the electoral machinery of a presidency whose public approval ratings are now under water. Without the support of the congressmen, Marcos Jr. has nothing. His electoral endorsement is, as things stand, a kiss of death.
But congressional support is contingent on the district-politicians getting their share of the gravy train. They need money to indulge in money politics.
Thirty-five of our most influential business associations have put out a statement demanding that the congressional insertions be stripped out. This is unprecedented. Over the next few days, spokesmen for civil society groups will flood media channels with horror stories about the congressional budget insertions. They will hold vigil over the budget hearings and the bicameral conference committee.
But money politics involves a deleterious symbiosis.

Debt payments ease to P1.65 trillion in 9 months
MANILA, Philippines — The country’s debt service bill fell by 11.7 percent to P1.65 trillion in the nine-month period ending September from last year’s P1.87 trillion, reflecting the timing of debt payments rather than the easing of the government’s debt pile.
Data from the Bureau of the Treasury showed that the amortization payments likewise dropped by 27 percent to P878.21 billion in the January-to-September period from P1.2 trillion in the same period a year ago.
Roughly 84 percent of the total amortization or P741.01 billion was remitted to domestic sources while the remaining P137.2 billion were paid to external creditors.
On the other hand, interest payments increased by 16 percent to P772.05 billion during the nine-month period from P665.85 billion the year prior.
About 76 percent of the interest payments or P584.74 billion were paid to domestic creditors.
Broken down, the government paid P404.52 billion in fixed-rate Treasury bonds, P131.24 billion in retail T-bonds and P41.15 billion in Treasury bills (T-bills).
It also settled P187.32 billion in interest owed to foreign financiers for the period ending September.
For September alone, the debt service bill stood at P207.46 billion, down by 36.7 percent from P327.89 billion in the same month in 2025.
As of September, the current debt payments represented 80.7 percent of the P2.05 trillion program this year, as indicated in the latest Budget of Expenditures and Sources of Financing document.
Principal amortization payments had reached 83.7 percent of the P1.05-trillion full-year allocation, while interest payments made up 77.5 percent of the P995.61-billion program.
Latest data showed that the Philippines’ outstanding debt climbed to a record P19.66 trillion as of end-September, due to additional borrowings and peso depreciation.
